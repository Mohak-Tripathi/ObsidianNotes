## 0 · Understanding the problem

💨 **What is [Gopuff](https://www.gopuff.com/)?**

Gopuff delivers goods typically found in a convenience store via **rapid delivery** and **500+ micro distribution centers (DCs)**.

---

## 1 · Requirements

### 1.1 Functional (what it does)

▸ **CRUX** — two features only.

1. ⭐ **Customers should be able to query availability of items, deliverable in 1 hour, by location.** ⭐⭐ **The parenthetical is the whole problem:** effective availability is the **union of all inventory in nearby DCs** — not one warehouse's stock.
2. ⭐ **Customers should be able to order multiple items at the same time.**

⚠ **Below the line (out of scope):**

- Handling **payments / purchases**.
- Handling **driver routing and deliveries**.
- **Search functionality and catalog APIs** — the system is _strictly_ concerned with availability and ordering.
- **Cancellations and returns.**

▹ ⭐ **HI's framing of the emphasis — quote it to yourself before you start:** _"aggregating availability of items across local distribution centers, and allowing users to place orders **without double booking**."_ ⚠ **In other problems you might care about catalog and search; here you deliberately do not.**

### 1.2 Non-functional (how it behaves)

- ⭐ **Availability requests fast — < 100ms** ▹ because they back use-cases like search, where a user types and expects results.
- ⭐⭐ **Ordering must be strongly consistent — two customers must not be able to purchase the same physical product.** ▹ This is the hard requirement of the problem, and it's a _correctness_ requirement, not a performance one.
- ⭐ **Scale — 10k DCs, 100k items** in the catalog across DCs.
- ⭐ **Order volume — O(10M orders/day).**

⚠ **Below the line:** privacy and security · disaster recovery.

> ⚠ **Two small source notes, so the numbers don't confuse you on a later pass:**
> 
> - HI's whiteboard shorthand writes **"Order volume O(1m)"** while the requirement above and **all the later math use 10M/day**. ⭐ **Use 10M** — it's what produces the 20k qps figure in §5.2.
> - ▹ **How to scope out-of-scope items:** HI notes interviewers are comfortable with you simply asserting _"I'm going to leave privacy out of scope to start"_ and will correct you if needed. **Assert, don't ask permission for each one.**

### 1.3 ⭐⭐ The design driver — one dataset, two opposite consistency requirements

> ⭐⭐ **State this before you draw anything. It is the organizing idea of the entire design, and every box you place afterwards is justified by it.**

- ▸ The **same inventory data** is hit by two paths with **contradictory** needs:

|Path|Volume|Consistency need|Therefore|
|---|---|---|---|
|**Availability (read)**|⭐ **~20k qps** (§5.2)|⚠ **staleness is fine** — a count a few seconds old is harmless|**read replicas + Redis cache, short TTL**|
|**Ordering (write)**|~100/sec (10M/day)|⭐⭐ **strongly consistent** — never sell the same physical item twice|**Postgres leader, single atomic transaction**|

- ⭐ **The read:write asymmetry is ~200:1**, so this is a Scaling Reads problem (`03d`) **on the read path only.** ⚠ **Do not let the cheap write path tempt you into weakening the consistency guarantee** — that's the one thing in this problem you cannot trade away.
- ⭐ **The resolution — say it like this:** _"I'll serve availability from read replicas and a cache because it tolerates staleness, and route orders to the leader in a single transaction because it can't. Same data, two paths, because they want opposite things."_
- ⊕ **(not in HI) — why this is the shape worth memorizing:** it recurs in nearly every inventory-shaped design. Browsing is cheap, high-volume and can lie slightly; committing is rare, expensive and must be exactly right. ⭐ **Whenever you see "lots of people look, few people take, and two must never take the same one," reach for this split immediately.**

### 1.4 Planning the approach

▸ ⭐ **HI makes this an explicit step — it's worth announcing in the room:**

- **Default approach:** build a system that satisfies the **functional** requirements first, with **little concern for scale**, then bring the **non-functional** concerns back **one at a time** in deep dives.
- **The order:** enumerate the **nouns** → build the **API** → **draw the boxes.**
- ▹ Saying this out loud costs 15 seconds and tells the interviewer you have a method rather than a habit.

## 2 · Core Entities

▸ **CRUX** — the high-level **nouns**. Not a full data model yet. ⭐ **Entities matter extra here because REST resources anchor on them** (`01b`) — getting them right is what makes the API fall out cleanly.

- **Item** — a _type_ of item, e.g. Cheetos. ⭐ **What customers actually care about** (catalog-level).
- **Inventory** — ⭐ **a physical instance of an item, located at a DC.** Summed across DCs to get the quantity available to a user for an Item.
- **DistributionCenter** — a physical location where items are stored; determines what's available to a user.
- **Order** — a collection of Inventory ordered by a user (+ shipping/billing).
- **OrderItem** — the line items of an order.

> ⭐⭐ **The distinction the whole design rests on — Item vs Inventory = Class vs Instance.**
> 
> - ▸ **Item is the class** ("Cheetos"); **Inventory is the instance** ("this bag of Cheetos, in DC 47").
> - ⭐ **Why it matters:** API consumers only ever see **Items**; the system must track **where the physical things are**. Availability = **sum Inventory rows across nearby DCs → report one quantity per Item.** ⚠ **Candidates who model a single "item with a stock count" cannot express the union-across-DCs requirement at all** — the design fails at requirement 1.
> - ⭐ **It's also what makes "don't double-book" expressible:** you lock/decrement a specific **Inventory** row, not an abstract Item.
> 
> ⭐ **HI's method for finding entities (reusable):** start with the most **concrete physical or business** entities (items, users, locations) and work **up** to the **abstract** ones (orders, carts). ▹ Working in that direction is what stops you missing one.

## 3 · API

▸ **CRUX** — tracks the functional requirements: **two endpoints, nothing more.** ⚠ **HI's warning:** there are endless extraneous details you _could_ add — **starting simple avoids burning time, and over-building the API is "a common mistake in practice."**

### 3.1 Availability — `GET /v1/availability`

```
GET /v1/availability?lat=LAT&long=LONG&keyword={}&page_size={}&page_num={}
->
{ items: [ { name: NAME, quantity: QTY } ] }
```

- ⭐ **Pagination** (`page_size`, `page_num`) — avoid overwhelming the client with more data than it needs. ▹ Same discipline as `05c §3.1`.

> ⚠ **Confusion resolved — `keyword` vs "search is out of scope."**
> 
> - ⚠ **Out of scope = the search _system_** — index, relevance ranking, typeahead. A separate service with its own store (`02b`), as §4.1's aside says.
> - ✅ **In scope = `keyword` as a plain _filter_** (a `WHERE` clause) on items **already** narrowed to nearby DCs.
> - ⭐ **The clue:** the NFR says <100ms _"to support use-cases like search"_ → something search-shaped **calls** this API; we don't **build** it. **The direction of the arrow is the distinction.**
> - ⭐⭐ **In the room:** safe to **drop it entirely**. If you keep it, one sentence — _"a filter on the nearby-DC results; real catalog search is a separate service."_ ⚠ **Unexplained, it reads as scope creep.**

### 3.2 Order — `POST /v1/order`

```
POST /v1/order
{ lat: LAT, long: LONG, items: ITEM1,ITEM2,ITEM3... }
->
Order | Failure
```

- ⭐⭐ **Note the location is passed to _both_ APIs — and say why:** before the order can be processed, the backend must **re-confirm the inventory is close enough to the user to deliver within 1 hour.** ⚠ **You cannot trust the availability response the client saw** — it may be stale by the time they check out, and it came from a cache. ▹ **This is the detail interviewers notice.**
- ▹ Returns `Order` **or** `Failure` — the failure case is load-bearing here (§4.2).

## 4 · High-Level Design

### 4.1) Customers should be able to query availability of items

▸ ⭐⭐ **Two steps, in this order — the ordering is the insight:**

1. **Find the DCs close enough to deliver in 1 hour.**
2. **Check the inventory of those DCs and return the union.**

⭐ **Why narrow by DC first:** **all inventory lives in a DC**, so filtering to serviceable DCs **shortcuts having to check every item in the system.** ▹ Both steps must be fast — the end-to-end budget is **<100ms**.

#### Nearby Service (first cut)

- **Approach:** an internal API — **lat/long in → list of DCs within 1 hour out.** Assume a **DC table** with lat/long; measure distance to the user's input with simple math.
    - **Basic:** Euclidean distance.
    - **Better:** ⭐ **Haversine formula** — accounts for the curvature of the Earth (`04a`).
    - A simple **threshold** on that distance gives DCs within X **"as the crow flies."**
- ⚠ **This does not actually satisfy the functional requirement** (1 hour of _drive time_, not distance). **Flag it and defer → DD1 (§5.1).**

```
Input (lat/long) ──► Nearby Service ──► DC Table
                                        DistributionCenter
                                          Id · Lat · Long
```

#### Availability Service

- **Approach:** given the DC IDs, query the **Inventory** table and **join** to the **Item** table (Postgres) to get name/description alongside the quantity.

```
                     ┌─ Inventory Table          Inventory
Availability ────────┤     [DC] → [{ITEM: QTY}]    ItemId · DCId · Quantity
Service              │
                     └─ Item Table               Item
                                                   Id · Name · Description
```

> ▹ **The aside worth raising unprompted (HI does):** in many e-commerce systems the **Catalog is stored separately from Inventory**, because they have **different consumers and different workloads**. HI keeps them in one DB here to stay simple and to match the stated requirements — but ⭐ **say to the interviewer that you'd ideally separate them and add a search index (Elasticsearch → `02b`) for catalog search.** Search is explicitly out of scope (§1.1), so this is a one-sentence callout, not a design.

#### Putting it together

```
User ──► API Gateway ──► Availability Service ──┬──► Nearby Service ──► DC Table
          (02d)                                 │         │
                                                │         └──► Travel Time Service (DD1)
                                                │
                                                └──► Inventory Table + Item Table
                                                     (replicated SQL)
```

- **Availability Service** — handles user requests for availability given a location.
- **Nearby Service** — syncs with the DC database and uses an **external Travel Time Service** to calculate travel times from DCs (potentially including traffic).
- **Inventory Table** — a **replicated** SQL table returning inventory per item and DC.

**Request flow — items A, B, C from latitude X, longitude Y:**

1. Request hits the **Availability Service** with location X, Y and any filters.
2. Availability Service calls the **Nearby Service** with X, Y.
3. Nearby Service returns the **list of DCs** that can deliver to that location.
4. Availability Service **queries the database** with those DC IDs.
5. **Sum the results** and return to the client.

### 4.2) Customers should be able to order items

▸ **The requirement:** **strong consistency** — two users must not order the same physical item. That means **check inventory, record the order, and update inventory _together, atomically_.**

- ▹ **Latency is not a big concern here** — users tolerate more latency at checkout than on browse. ⭐ **Spend the budget on correctness.**
- ⭐⭐ **Name the general problem:** _"this is **double booking**, which shows up across system design problems."_ The fix is some form of **locking** — hold the inventory while checking it and recording the order, such that **only one user can hold it at a time.** ▹ **Full rung ladder → `03b`.**

#### ⚠ Bad Solution — separate databases for orders and inventory

- **Approach:** orders in one store, inventory in another. On order: **lock the inventory records → create the order record → decrement inventory → release the lock.**
- ⚠ **What kind of lock — and this is what makes the solution bad.** Not a DB row lock: `SELECT … FOR UPDATE` can't span two stores, so it must be an **application-held distributed lock that outlives any single transaction** (`03b` rung 5) — a **Redis lease with a TTL**, `reserved_by` + `reserved_until` columns on the inventory record, or ZooKeeper/etcd. ⭐ **One lock per item**, so a multi-item order holds several at once → hold-and-wait → the deadlock below, and **no DB deadlock detector** to kill a victim. ⚠ A Redis lock is also **efficiency, not correctness** (`03b`, `02a §2`) — a stall past the TTL double-grants the inventory. ⭐⭐ **Contrast the Great solution: the transaction _is_ the lock** — the engine takes and releases row locks, detects deadlocks, and rolls back atomically, for free.
- ✅ **The genuine upside (say it, so the rejection sounds considered):** you can use **the best data store for each use case** — e.g. a key-value store for inventory, a relational DB for orders.
- ⚠ **Challenges — "some nasty failure modes":**
    1. ⭐ **Partial failure.** The service **crashes after creating the order but before decrementing inventory** → a later user orders inventory already promised to the first. ▸ You now need to **sweep for these failures and reverse them.**
    2. ⭐ **Deadlock on overlapping orders.** User1 and User2 both want items A and B; **User1 holds A, User2 holds B** → neither can proceed.
- ▸ ⚠ **HI's verdict:** if you go this route, **you must be able to address each failure mode.** ⭐ **Which is exactly why it loses — the complexity you take on is the _recovery_ machinery, not the feature.**

> ⊕ **(not in HI) — the fixes, if an interviewer makes you defend this path.** Both are in `03b`:
> 
> - **Deadlock → ordered locking.** Acquire locks in a **globally sorted order** (e.g. by item ID), never "initiator first." Plus DB deadlock detection and retry the loser.
> - **Partial failure → a saga / orchestrator** with compensating actions (`03c`), or a distributed transaction across stores (`03g`).
> - ⭐ **The point to make:** both fixes are real, and both are **more machinery than the single-database answer**. That comparison is the argument, not a vague "it's complex."

**Why climb:** every failure mode here comes from the state being split across two stores. Put it in one and they all disappear.

#### ⭐ Great Solution — one database, one transaction, `SERIALIZABLE`

- **Approach:** put **orders and inventory in the same Postgres database** and lean on **ACID**. A **single transaction** at isolation level **`SERIALIZABLE`** makes the whole operation atomic → if two users order the same item at once, **one is rejected**, because the transaction **fails to commit when the inventory isn't available.**

```
Orders Service ──Write Transaction──► Postgres Leader ──┬── Inventory Table
                                                        └── Orders Table
```

**The order flow:**

1. User requests an order for items A, B, C from the **Orders Service**.
2. Orders Service creates **one transaction**, submitted to the **Postgres leader**:
    - **a.** Check inventory for A, B, C **> 0**.
    - **b.** If **any** item is out of stock → **the transaction fails.**
    - **c.** If all in stock → record the order and set inventory status for A, B, C to **"ordered."**
    - **d.** New row in the **Orders** table (and **OrderItems**).
    - **e.** **Commit.**
3. On success, return the order to the user.

- ⚠ **Challenges (name them — this isn't free):**
    - **Partly couples the scaling** of inventory and orders.
    - **Can't use the best data store for each use case.**
- ⭐⭐ **The principle to quote:** _"When atomicity of transactions is a requirement, it's helpful to have your data **colocated in an ACID data store**."_ Cross-store transactions are possible and sometimes wanted for other reasons, but **the overhead isn't what this interview is about.**
- ⚠ **The honest downside to raise yourself:** if **any** item in the order is unavailable, **the whole order fails.** ⭐ **Return a meaningful error naming the item** — and note this is **preferable to partially succeeding**, which could produce a nonsensical order (HI's example: **a device without its battery**). ▹ Raising this unprompted is a product-thinking signal.

> ⊕ **(not in HI) — a rung-level refinement worth having ready, because a sharp interviewer will probe it.** Your own `03b` escalation order is **conditional write → OCC → pessimistic → SERIALIZABLE → distributed lock**, reaching for the simplest that fits. HI jumps straight to `SERIALIZABLE` (rung 4).
> 
> - ⭐ **A transaction of conditional decrements achieves the same guarantee at a weaker isolation level:**
>     
>     ```sql
>     UPDATE inventory SET quantity = quantity - :n
>     WHERE item_id = :a AND dc_id = :dc AND quantity >= :n;   -- rowCount 0 ⇒ abort
>     ```
>     
> - ▸ Each `UPDATE` is an **atomic check-and-write on the contended row**, so there's no lost update and **no serialization-failure retry storm** under load. Run all three inside one transaction; any `rowCount = 0` → roll back the whole order.
>     
> - ⭐ **When SERIALIZABLE genuinely earns it:** **write skew** — a cross-row invariant with no single shared row to guard (`03b` rung 4). Here each Inventory row **is** the contended thing, so rung 1 suffices.
>     
> - ⚠ **How to play it:** **give HI's answer first** — "one transaction, SERIALIZABLE" is a recognized, strong answer — **then offer the conditional-decrement version as the production refinement.** ▹ Same tactic as `05c §5.1`: lead with the expected answer, add the sharper one.
>     

### 4.3 The initial solution (what you should have on the board)

```
User ──► API Gateway ──┬──► Availability Service ──► Nearby Service ──► DC Table
                       │              │
                       │              └──► [read replicas] Inventory + Item
                       │
                       └──► Orders Service ──Write Transaction──► [leader] Inventory + Orders
```

- **Three services:** **Availability**, **Orders**, and a **shared Nearby Service.** ⭐ **Both** Availability and Orders use Nearby to find DCs close enough to the user.
- **One Postgres database** for inventory and orders, **partitioned by region.**
- ⭐ **Availability reads via read replicas; Orders writes to the leader** using atomic transactions to avoid double writes.
- ▹ HI: _"A great foundation!"_ — and note the read-replica/leader split was already implied by §1.3. **Deep dives now harden both paths.**

## 5 · Deep Dives

> ▸ Two dives, one per path — and they map exactly onto the two halves of §1.3. ⭐ **HI's senior bar is explicit that you must have optimized solutions for _both_: the atomic order transaction and the availability scaling.**

### 1) Make availability lookups incorporate traffic and drive time ⭐

- ▸ **The problem:** nearby DCs are currently found by **straight-line distance**. ⚠ But a DC **over a river or across a border** can be close in miles and far in drive time — and **traffic** changes travel times through the day. The FR says **1 hour of drive time**, so distance alone doesn't satisfy it.

#### ⚠ Bad Solution — simple SQL distance

- **Approach:** all DC lat/longs in a table; compute distance to the user's input with simple math (**Euclidean**, or **Haversine** for the Earth's curvature → `04a`); take a **threshold** → DCs within X as the crow flies.
- ⚠ **Challenges:** no account of **traffic or road conditions**; and no account of **multiple DCs in the same city.**

**Why climb:** distance is a proxy for the thing we actually promised. Measure the real thing.

#### Good Solution — sync DCs to memory, query a travel-time service

- ⭐ **The enabling observation:** **DCs rarely change — they're buildings.** So **sync periodically (e.g. every 5 minutes) from the DC table into the service's memory.** ▹ Static data belongs in memory; don't pay a query per request for data that changes monthly.
- **Approach:** on each request, use a **Travel Time Estimation Service** to compute travel time from the input location to **each** DC, **iterating over all of them.**
- ⚠ **Challenge:** ⭐ **far too many calls to the travel-time service.** Most DCs **could never plausibly** deliver within 1 hour, so almost all the work is wasted.

**Why climb:** we're paying for exact answers on candidates we could have rejected for free.

#### ⭐ Great Solution — radius prune, then travel time

- **Approach:** keep the periodic in-memory sync, then on each request:
    1. **Prune to candidates** with a **fixed radius — say 60 miles**, the most optimistic distance drivable in 1 hour.
    2. Pass **only those candidates** to the external **travel-time service** for the final estimate.

```
Input Position ──► Nearby Service ──(synced every 5 min)──► DC Table
                        │
                        └──Query *close* candidates only──► Travel Time Service ──► Nearby DCs
```

- ▹ ⊕ **What the travel-time service actually is:** a **matrix/batch routing API** — **Google Maps Distance Matrix**, **Mapbox Matrix** or **HERE Matrix Routing** — one origin × many destinations per call, returning `duration_in_traffic`. At scale you self-host an open-source engine (**OSRM**, **Valhalla**) on OpenStreetMap data, or run an in-house ETA service over a road graph plus live traffic, as Uber/DoorDash do. ⭐ **Billing is per origin-destination pair — which is exactly why the radius prune pays for itself.**
- ⭐⭐ **Name the shape:** this is the **`04a` law** — _the cheap filter only hands you candidates; you always finish with exact math._ Here the "exact math" is an **external API call**, so the candidate set isn't just a latency win, it's a **cost and quota win.** ▹ Saying that connection out loud is a strong move.

### 2) Make availability lookups fast and scalable ⭐⭐

> ⭐⭐ **Lead this dive with the arithmetic, not the solution.** HI: _"Using quantitative estimation where you've spotted a potential bottleneck can significantly improve your interview performance"_ — it gives you and the interviewer **a common set of data from which to weigh tradeoffs**, and shows you can make reasonable assumptions.

#### ⭐⭐ The derivation — 10M orders/day → 20k queries/sec

- **The assumptions (state them as assumptions):**
    - **10M orders/day** (from the NFRs).
    - Each user views about **10 pages** — search, homepage, etc. — before purchasing.
    - Only about **5%** of users actually buy; the rest are just shopping.

```
Queries = 10M orders/day ÷ 100k seconds/day × 10 ÷ 0.05 = 20k queries/second
```

- ▹ **Read it in plain terms:** 10M/day ≈ **~100 orders/sec**; at 5% conversion each order implies **20 shoppers**, each viewing **10 pages** → **200 page-views per order** → **~20k qps**.
- ▹ **`100k seconds/day`** is the standard approximation for 86,400 (`01i`) — use it, it's faster and nobody objects.
- ⭐ **This is a sizeable number** and it's what makes the read path the main engineering problem. ⚠ **Contrast it with the write path: ~100 orders/sec.** The 200:1 asymmetry from §1.3 is now a measured fact, not an assertion.

▸ ⭐ **Pattern: Scaling Reads (`03d`).** Inventory queries vastly outnumber purchases — 20k qps of availability checks against only occasional inventory updates — so **aggressive caching with short TTLs becomes critical.**

#### Good Solution — Redis cache with a low TTL

- **Approach:** add **Redis**. Availability Service checks the cache for a given set of inputs → **hit: return it; miss: query the DB, then write the result into the cache.** Set a **low TTL (e.g. 1 minute)** so results stay fresh.
- ⚠ **Challenge — ⭐ cache coherence, and note who owns it:** the cache must stay up to date with the inventory table. ⭐⭐ **The Orders Service must expire the affected cache entries when it writes to the inventory table.** ▹ **The writer owns invalidation, not the reader.** A TTL alone bounds staleness but doesn't stop you selling something you just sold.

> ⚠ **Confusion resolved — cache _or_ replicas? Both. They do different jobs.**
> 
> - ⭐ **Cache removes the work** (stores the computed answer — one key lookup, no join or aggregate). **Replicas absorb what's left** (misses, cold start) by recomputing per request. **Partitioning shrinks what each node holds.**
> - ⭐ **The relationship:** a good hit rate **sizes down the replica fleet.** At 20k qps and 90% hits, **2k qps still lands somewhere** — and it shouldn't be the leader taking order transactions.
> - ⭐⭐ **Key the cache by region, not raw lat/long** — coordinates are near-unique per user (terrible hit rate); a zip-3 region is shared by thousands.
> - ⚠ **Source note:** HI's text calls caching "critical" but its final DD2 diagram shows **only replicas + sharding.** §6 here keeps both, deliberately.

**Why climb:** a cache absorbs repeat reads but every miss still lands on one database, and all 20k qps still target the same dataset.

#### ⭐ Great Solution — partition by region + read replicas

- ⭐⭐ **Partitioning — the "trivial partitioning" senior candidates are expected to spot:** since you **only ever read inventory from a nearby collection of DCs**, group DCs into a **region ID using the first 3 digits of their zipcode**, and **partition inventory on region ID.**
    - ⭐ **Payoff:** a query touches **mostly 1 or 2 partitions** instead of the entire inventory dataset. ▹ **The access pattern _is_ the partition key** — the cleanest possible sharding story (`01f`).
- ⭐ **Read replicas** for availability, since it **tolerates a small amount of inconsistency** (`02g §5`). ⚠ **Orders still go to the Postgres leader** — strong consistency is non-negotiable there.
- ⚠ **Challenges:** manage **replica sizing against traffic**, and manage **partitioning so no single replica is overloaded.**

> ⚠ **Confusion resolved — "partitioning" or "sharding"? And what happens when we eventually must shard?**
> 
> - ⭐ **Here it is _partitioning_:** one instance, `PARTITION BY LIST (region_id)` → **pruning** on `WHERE region_id = …`, smaller per-partition indexes, and ⭐⭐ **transactions span partitions normally, so the atomic order is untouched.** **Sharding** means **separate instances** — ⚠ say that word and you've invited _"so what happened to your transaction?"_
> - ⭐ **The numbers say partitioning suffices:** inventory ≈ 10k DCs × ~3k SKUs ≈ **30M rows ≈ 3GB**; writes ≈ **100/sec**. ⭐ **It buys query locality, not capacity.**
> - ⭐ **What eventually forces real sharding is the ORDERS table, not inventory** — 10M/day ≈ **3.65B rows/year**, while inventory stays flat at ~3GB (current state). ⭐ **Fix = time-partition + archive cold months**, not region shards. Different problem, different tool.
> - ⭐⭐ **If you do shard, shard by the key that bounds your transactions.** Region keeps the inventory invariant inside one shard (`03b`: _sharding splits rows, never one row_). ⚠ **The hole — boundary straddle:** a 1-hour radius can cross two zip-3 regions. Three outs: **constrain the order to one region** · **split it per fulfillment region** (Amazon → shipments; all-or-nothing becomes a _product_ decision) · **coarser regions.**
> - ⭐ **Even cross-shard you don't need 2PC:** only each **single inventory-row decrement** must be atomic, and a row never splits. Item 3 of 5 fails ⇒ **release the 4 you took** = compensation/saga (`03c`), not a distributed transaction (`03g`).
> - ⭐ **The one-liner:** _"Partition by region now for locality; don't shard until a number forces it — and when it does, region is the right key because it keeps the invariant inside one shard."_

AND quantity >= n`** (rowCount 0 ⇒ abort) = atomic check+write on the contended row, **no serialization-failure retries**. SERIALIZABLE truly needed for **write skew** (no shared row). ⚠ **give HI's answer first, then offer this**|
