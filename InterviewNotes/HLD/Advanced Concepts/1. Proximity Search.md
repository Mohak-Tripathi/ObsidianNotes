**Why this exists** _(HI's framing, in brief):_

- ⭐ **Proximity search = searching by location instead of by ID or value** — drivers near a rider, restaurants near a user, people near a place. Oddly specific, but a **regular in system-design interviews.**

## 1 · Why a plain index fails ⭐

> ⭐ **This is the entire "aha" of the chapter.** If you can explain §1 cleanly and nothing else, you've scored most of the available points.

### 1.1 The age query is fast — why

▸ **CRUX** — a B-tree keeps sorted keys **packed together on disk** (`01d`). `20` sits next to `21` next to `22`, all on the same page. _"Everyone between 20 and 30"_ = **one seek + a short sequential read.** About the easiest thing you can ask a database.

### 1.2 ⭐ Location breaks this — the root problem

▸ **CRUX** — location is **two numbers** (lat, long) and what you care about is the **straight-line distance** between points. ⚠ **A B-tree sorts on one number at a time.**

- **Index on latitude** → you get a **horizontal strip** of the earth.
    
- **Index on longitude** → you get a **vertical strip.**
    
- ⚠ **A composite index on both doesn't save you** — a B-tree sorts by the first column and only **breaks ties** with the second, so it's _still_ effectively a single-dimension sort. The strip it pulls back holds **millions of rows, none ranked by distance** from the rider.
    
- ▸ So it falls back to **brute force:** compute real distance for every row, discard everyone past 2 km.
    
- ⭐ **The one-line root cause:** ⭐ **a one-dimensional sort order can't preserve two-dimensional closeness.** Flatten the map onto a single sorted axis and **points that are neighbours on the ground can land far apart in the index.**
    
- ▹ **Why this matters as a callback:** `01d` taught you _why_ B-trees are fast (packed sorted pages). This is the **first place that strength becomes a weakness** — the very packing that makes range scans cheap is 1-D by construction. **Same structure, wrong dimensionality.**
    
- ▸ **The rest of the field is just two ways to recover enough of that lost closeness** to make an index useful again.

## 3 · The two approaches — the decision ⭐

> ⭐ **This is the framing that scores.** Everything in §4 and §5 is an instance of one of these two, and **the shape of your data picks which.**

### 3.1 ⭐ The decision axis — data shape

|Your data|Reach for|Because|Systems|
|---|---|---|---|
|⭐ **Geometric** — polygons, roads, delivery zones; **containment / intersection** matters|**Custom spatial tree**|the DB must **understand shapes**|**PostGIS** (`02g`), **Elasticsearch** (`02b`)|
|⭐ **Mostly points that move** — drivers, live users, devices|**Encoded key** (flatten to one sortable value)|you want **cheap writes + fast candidate generation**, not polygon math|**Redis geo** (`02a`), **geohash / S2 / H3**|

- ⭐ **The mnemonic to walk out loud:** ⭐ **Spatial tree for shapes. Encoded cells for moving points. And always, always post-filter.**
- ▸ **Once you know which approach a system takes, its tradeoffs fall right out** — trees pay for shape-understanding with pricier writes; encoded keys pay for cheap writes by being points-only + ring-querying (§5.1a).

#### 3.1a ⚠ Confusion resolved — why _exactly_ two, not a menu

> **Doubt (essence):** why only two approaches — is this just two popular picks, or is it forced?

- ⭐ **It's forced, not a curated list.** The two approaches are the **two exhaustive answers to one binary question:** given §1's fixed fact — _a 1-D sort can't preserve 2-D closeness_ — **do I keep using an ordinary sorted index, or not?**
    - **Keep it** → then I _must_ flatten (lat, long) into one key, engineered so closeness mostly survives → **encoded keys.** A B-tree sorts one number, so you must produce one number.
    - **Abandon it** → then I _must_ build a new structure that reasons about 2-D **regions** directly → **custom spatial tree.**
- ⚠ **No third branch** — it's a yes/no. Everything else is a **sub-choice inside a branch:** quadtree vs k-d vs R-tree = "which tree" (all branch 1); geohash vs S2 vs H3 = "which flattening" (all branch 2). Never a new top-level move.
- ⭐ **Why data shape picks the branch — it decides which is even _applicable_:**
    - **Shapes (a Bihar-sized polygon, a road) force branch 1** — you _cannot_ flatten a sprawling shape to one point-key without destroying it; containment/intersection needs a structure that holds the shape.
    - **Points (drivers) fit branch 2** — a point flattens cleanly, and the dominant cost is **write throughput** (one integer update per move), which trees pay dearly for and encoded keys don't.
- ⭐ **The trap to avoid:** the trigger is **point vs shape**, _not_ big-area vs small-area. A polygon forces a tree whether it's one state or the whole planet; point data makes **both** applicable, and write-pattern breaks the tie.
- ⊕ **(not in HI) The scope of "only two":** this holds for **low-dimensional** (2-D lat/long) proximity. In _high_ dimensions (embedding vectors) both break, and you move to approximate-nearest-neighbour indexes — a different problem (Vector Databases, `04c`), not a hidden third option here.

## 4 · Custom spatial trees ⚠

> ⚠ **The whole of §4 is `(⚠ below the interview waterline — knowledge only)` for your profile.** Read it **once** for intuition — the four structures form a clean chain of fixes, which makes it cheap to hold — but **do not memorize the split mechanics.** For a Swiggy design you'll reach for encoded cells (§5) ~90% of the time. I've kept it compact deliberately.
> 
> ▸ **The one transferable idea across all three:** to work **as a database index** (not just in RAM), a spatial tree must be **shaped like a B-tree on disk** — balanced, page-sized nodes, predictable depth. Every structure below is judged on how well it hits that bar.

### 4.1 Quadtrees — the intuition builder

▸ **Turn the map itself into a tree.** Whole map = root. Split into **4 quadrants** → 4 children. Any quadrant with too many points **splits into 4 again**, recursing until every **leaf** holds few enough points to scan. Points live **only in leaves.** (Four-way split = the name.)

- **Search:** walk down from root, at each node compare query point to the cell's midpoint (N/S, E/W), drop into the matching quadrant, reach a leaf. **Candidate set = that leaf + neighbouring leaves** (so you don't miss someone just across a boundary) → exact distance math picks winners.
    
- ⭐ **Nice property — density-adaptive:** dense Manhattan → deep subtree; empty lake → one coarse cell. **No space wasted subdividing empty water.**
    
- ⚠ **Problem 1 — depth / uneven latency.** Splits land at the **geometric midpoint**, never where the data sits. A dense cluster keeps getting **halved** until points separate — 10+ levels downtown, 2 levels in rural Vermont. ⚠ **Latency depends on where you look, and the busiest regions are slowest** → hard to reason about worst case.
    
- ⚠ **Problem 2 — disk.** A quadtree is a **pointer structure**: every node at an arbitrary address holding child pointers. Fine in RAM (pointer = cheap memory lookup); ⚠ **a DB reads fixed-size pages from disk, and each pointer risks a random read** for an unloaded page. A few hops in, you're mostly waiting on disk.
    
- ▸ **Verdict:** great **in memory** (Google Maps tiles, game-engine collision) — but not sturdy enough for a disk-resident index that overflows RAM.
    

#### 4.1a ⚠ Confusion resolved — what "data"/"density" mean, and why the cut is blind

> **Doubt (essence):** what does a quadtree actually store, what is "density," and how is the depth decided if splits always land at the midpoint?

- ⭐ **The data = a pile of `(lat, long, payload)` points**, one record per thing indexed (e.g. `(40.71, −74.00, driver_8813)`). **Points live only in leaves;** internal nodes are just "this square → 4 sub-squares + pointers."
- ⭐ **Density = points per map area** — literally how many records fall inside a square. Manhattan = thousands of drivers in a small box; a lake = ~zero. Not a census sense.
- ⭐ **Two separate decisions — this is the whole knot:**
    - **Where to cut** → **always the geometric midpoint, blind to the points.** Not your choice, never at the crowd.
    - **Whether to cut again** → **only if the square holds more than N points.** This is the density-driven part.
- ⭐ **So nobody decides the depth — it emerges.** Apply one dumb rule (_over N points → halve the square 4 ways_) and dense regions recurse many times while empty ones stop at depth 1. **The tree adapts to density even though every individual cut ignores the data.**
- ⚠ **Why midpoint-blind is the flaw:** a lopsided cluster (all points jammed top-left) still gets a dead-center cut → **the other 3 quadrants come back empty, but you still had to make that cut to get at the crowded one** → you keep halving the crowded quarter, burning wasted levels. Depth becomes location-dependent → the k-d tree's **median** split (§4.2) exists to fix exactly this.

#### 4.1b ⚠ Confusion resolved — why the disk problem is fatal (vs a B-tree)

> **Doubt (essence):** why is "on disk" such a big deal, and how does a B-tree (`01d`) dodge it while a quadtree can't?

- ⭐ **Two stacking penalties, not one.** **Depth** (Problem 1) and the **pointer structure** (Problem 2) compound: pointers already mean random IO per hop, and depth makes each query pay that IO more times.
- ⭐ **The real indictment is fan-out.** A B-tree node = **one disk page packed with ~hundreds of keys → hundreds of children.** So depth is `log₂₅₆(N)`: **16 M rows in ~3 levels → ~3 page reads for any row.** A quadtree has **fan-out 4** (often effectively worse when lopsided) → `log₄(N)`, and Manhattan needs **10+ levels.**
- ⚠ **So per lookup:** quadtree = **~10 random disk seeks** (each node at an arbitrary address); B-tree = **~3 packed-page reads.** ⭐ **"How many random seeks to reach a leaf" IS Problem 2.**
- ⭐ **Depth: controlled vs at-the-mercy-of-data.** B-tree grows **bottom-up, self-balancing** — leaf overflows → splits → pushes a key up → **every leaf stays the same depth**, guaranteed shallow. Quadtree grows **top-down by geometry** — depth is **whatever density forces**, no mechanism holds it shallow.
- ▸ **This is exactly why §4.3's R-tree wins:** it rebuilds the spatial idea to be **balanced like a B-tree** — every leaf same depth, every node one page. Your comparison here _is_ the motivation for R-trees.

### 4.2 k-d trees → BKD trees — fixing the split

▸ **k-d tree = the binary cousin.** Instead of 4-way splits, it **alternates dimensions**: level 1 splits on x, level 2 on y, level 3 back to x… cycling one dimension per level.

- ⭐ **The one difference that matters — median split, not midpoint.** Picture 10 drivers on a street, 9 bunched east, 1 straggler west.
    - **Quadtree** cuts the **geometric middle** → lone western driver on one side, all 9 crammed on the other → the crowded half must split again and again, each cut slicing empty space. _(That's the Manhattan depth problem.)_
    - **k-d tree** cuts at the **median** (the 5th driver from west) → the line lands **in the thick of the crowd** where separating actually needs to happen → 5 per side however lopsided the street.
- ⭐ **Every cut halves the number of _points_, not the _area_** → keeps the tree balanced at depth **log(n)** whether downtown or empty highway.
- ⚠ **Inherits the disk problem** — still pointers all the way down, no clean page mapping. Fast in RAM, painful past it.

▸ **BKD tree (block k-d tree) = the disk fix.** Pack points into **blocks sized to a disk page**; build the whole tree **once from a batch.**

- ⚠ **The constraint that matters:** a BKD tree is essentially **write-once** — built for a chunk of data that **doesn't change.** ▸ Hold this for the "moving drivers" tradeoff (§6): write-once + constantly-moving points is a bad fit.
- ⭐ **This is what Elasticsearch uses for geo fields today** (`02b`). An ES geo query is the **k-d tree idea repackaged into a disk-friendly block-structured index.**

#### 4.2a ⚠ Confusion resolved — visualizing the cut (quadtree vs k-d) and what BKD changes

> **Doubt (essence):** hard to picture — with the "9 east, 1 west" drivers, how does the quadtree cut vs the k-d cut, and what does "BKD changes what the leaf is" actually mean?

**Part 1 — the cut, on a number line** (drivers on a street, `x = 0..100`, leaf capacity 2):

```
•                                              • • • • • • • • •
x=1                                            x=90 .......... 98
```

- **Quadtree — cuts the fixed geometric midpoint, blind to points:**

```
cut@50 : [0—50) 1pt✓   [50—100] 9pts→recurse
cut@75 :               [50—75) EMPTY   [75—100] 9pts→recurse
cut@87 :                              [75—87) EMPTY  [87—100] 9pts→recurse ...
```

⚠ cuts keep slicing **EMPTY** cells west of the crowd → ~5–6 wasted levels before 9 points separate.

- **k-d — cuts at the median point, so each cut halves the COUNT:**

```
sorted: 1, 90,91,92,93 | 94,95,96,97,98   ← cut between 5th & 6th
        [ 5 pts ]         [ 5 pts ]        ← straggler just rides in the left half
```

⭐ no empty cells; the left cell is geometrically **wide** but holds only 5 points — **depth tracks point-count, not width** → balanced `log(n)`.

- ⭐ **One-liner:** quadtree = **cut the space in half** (empty halves, depth chases the crowd); k-d = **cut the points in half** (no empty halves, depth = log(points)).

**Part 2 — what BKD changes: the leaf, nothing else.** Splitting stays identical median cuts. The fix is what sits at the bottom.

- ⚠ **Plain k-d — leaf = ONE point**, each at a random disk address:

```
   [cut] → [cut] → (•)(•)(•)(•)   each (•) = 1 point @ random address
```

Reaching points = pointer-hop → random seek per hop, and you get **one point per seek** → the `4.1b` disk-death.

- ⭐ **BKD — leaf = a PAGE-sized BLOCK of many points**, packed contiguously:

```
   [cut] → [cut] → ┌───────┐┌───────┐   each box = 1 disk page,
                   │•••••••││•••••••│      ~hundreds of points packed in
                   └───────┘└───────┘
```

⭐ **one seek → one page → hundreds of candidates at once**, instead of one seek → one point. Same seeks, hundreds× the payload.

|leaf holds|to read ~800 nearby points|
|---|---|
|plain k-d — **1 point**|~800 random seeks ⚠|
|BKD — **~a page**|~1 page read ⭐|

- ⭐ **This is the identical fan-out / page-packing fix a B-tree uses** (`4.1b`) — a B-tree leaf isn't one key either, it's a packed page. BKD applies that to k-d's spatial split.
- ⚠ **The catch — write-once.** Packing points into balanced page-blocks needs **all points up front**, sorted, laid out in one batch pass. Adding one driver later can overflow a block and cascade the balance → in practice you **rebuild**. Fine for static docs (**Elasticsearch**, `02b`); a driver moving every few seconds → constant rebuilds → so moving points go to **encoded cells** (§5, one cheap integer update per move).
- ▸ **The chain:** quadtree (blind midpoint → wasted depth) → k-d (median → balanced, but pointer/disk-hostile) → BKD (median + page-packed blocks → disk-friendly, but write-once).

### 4.3 R-trees — shapes + disk, done right

▸ **The first spatial index built from the ground up for on-disk DB use.** Solved two problems the earlier trees couldn't.

- ⭐ **Problem 1 — shapes.** Earlier trees assumed your data is a **point** (a driver, a pin). Real geo data isn't: a **highway is a line**, a **county is a polygon**, and even a Starbucks (a point) has a **delivery zone that's a polygon.** You can't drop a county into one quadtree cell — it **sprawls across many.**
- ⭐ **Problem 2 — disk.** Needs the spatial equivalent of a B-tree: stays balanced as data changes, reads fast off disk, updates cheaply when a driver moves.

▸ **The R-tree's answer — the minimum bounding rectangle (MBR).** Wrap every object in the **smallest axis-aligned rectangle** that fully contains it.

- A **point** → a degenerate zero-size rectangle.
    
- A **highway** → a long thin sliver.
    
- A **county** → a big sprawling box.
    
- Then **nearby rectangles group inside a larger enclosing rectangle**, groups nest into bigger ones, all the way to the root.
    
- ⭐ **Why it works as a DB index:** **balanced exactly like a B-tree** — every leaf at the **same depth** (any query touches the same predictable number of pages), every node sized to **one disk page.** Inserts/deletes rebalance by **splitting and merging pages**, same as a regular B-tree.
    
- ⚠ **The tradeoff to watch — overlap.** Unlike quadtree cells, **R-tree rectangles can overlap.** If your query point lands inside **two** bounding rectangles, you must **descend both subtrees.** Sloppy insertion → lots of overlap → lots of branches → performance drags.
    
    - ▸ **Fix — the R*-tree:** a refinement that keeps overlap low via **smarter insertion heuristics.** Almost every modern R-tree implementation is built on these. _(Heuristic details = pure name-drop, skip.)_
- ⭐ **Where it ships:** **PostGIS** builds spatial indexes on Postgres's **GiST framework** → R-tree-style bounding-box behaviour (`02g`); SQLite and Oracle Spatial ship their own R-tree variants. ▸ **If your problem is real geometry** — _"does this delivery zone contain this address," "does this road cross this county"_ — **this is the approach.**
    

#### 4.3a ⚠ Confusion resolved — HOW the rectangle solves both problems (and why height stays uniform)

> **Doubt (essence):** I get that R-trees solve shapes + predictable reads that quadtree/k-d/BKD can't — but _how_? "They draw overlapping rectangles and it works" isn't an explanation. And how does the height stay the same?

**What the rectangle is, concretely.** Every object gets wrapped in its **minimum bounding rectangle (MBR)** — the smallest axis-aligned box that fully contains it:

```
a point (driver)   →  ▫   zero-size box (just the point)
a road (line)      →  ▭   long thin box around the line
a county (polygon) →  ⬛  big box around the whole county
```

Nearby boxes **group into a bigger box**, those groups into still-bigger boxes, up to the root — boxes-inside-boxes:

```
              ┌──────────── R (root box, covers all) ────────────┐
        ┌───── A (NYC) ─────┐              ┌───── B (Boston) ─────┐
    ┌── A1 ──┐   ┌── A2 ──┐            ┌── B1 ──┐   ┌── B2 ──┐
    │ •• road│   │county ⬛│            │ •••    │   │ • ▭    │
    └────────┘   └────────┘            └────────┘   └────────┘
```

Each box stores **only its own coordinates** (min/max lat/long) + pointers to the boxes inside it. That's the whole structure.

**Problem 1 (shapes) — the box solves it.**

- ⚠ **WRONG:** "the index must understand the actual shape of a county."
- ⭐ **CORRECT:** the tree walk never touches the real shape — it only compares **rectangles.** The messy county collapses to a simple box, and _box-vs-box_ is trivial arithmetic.
- ▸ Quadtree/k-d **couldn't** hold a county because they store **points**, and a county isn't at one (lat,long) — it sprawls, no single cell to file it in. The R-tree stores **a box around the county** instead, and a box _does_ have clean coordinates. ⭐ **So a county, a road, and a driver are all the same thing to the index — a rectangle.** Don't index the shape; index a box around it.

**Problem 2 (predictable disk reads) — the box solves it too.** A query is one cheap test — **"does my search area overlap this box?"** — four comparisons, no geometry:

```
overlap ⟺ A.minLat ≤ B.maxLat AND A.maxLat ≥ B.minLat
          AND A.minLng ≤ B.maxLng AND A.maxLng ≥ B.minLng
```

Walk a "find everything near me" query down the tree:

```
At root R:  overlap A? YES → descend A
            overlap B? NO  → ⭐ SKIP all of B and everything inside it
Inside A:   overlap A1? YES → descend    overlap A2? NO → skip the county subtree
Inside A1:  leaves = the CANDIDATE SET → NOW do exact distance/geometry math
```

- ⭐ **The overlap test is the pruning engine.** One "NO" lops off an entire branch — those pages are never read. Same discipline as a B-tree lookup ("key > 500 → whole left subtree irrelevant"), except the comparison is **box-overlap** instead of number-ordering.
- ⭐ **Disk specifically:** every node = **one disk page**, every leaf at the **same depth**, kept balanced by page split/merge (`4.1b`). So any query touches a **shallow, predictable** number of page reads, whichever region — unlike the quadtree's uncontrolled depth + random-address pointer hops.

**The one honest cost — overlap.** Unlike quadtree cells, R-tree sibling boxes **can overlap.** If your query point sits inside both A and B:

```
   ┌────A────┐
   │     ┌───┼───B───┐
   │  ✕  │   │       │   ✕ in BOTH → must descend BOTH branches
   └─────┼───┘       │
         └───────────┘
```

⚠ More overlap → more double-descents → slower. That's the R-tree's characteristic tax; the **R*-tree** is just smarter insertion rules that keep overlap low. _(Heuristics = name-drop, skip.)_

- ⭐ **One-sentence answer:** wrap every object in a rectangle, and rectangle-overlap is a trivial 4-comparison test that (a) makes messy shapes indexable and (b) prunes whole subtrees so reads stay B-tree-shallow. **The rectangle does all the work.**

**Why the height stays uniform — it grows UPWARD, like a B-tree.**

- ⭐ **Nodes only ever split; they never deepen locally.** Insert into a full page → it **splits into two** and pushes a pointer **up** to the parent; if the parent overflows it splits and pushes up too; if that reaches the root, you make **a new root on top** → the whole tree gains one level **everywhere at once.**
- ⭐ **So every leaf is always the same number of hops from the root** — there's no way to lengthen one branch locally (a leaf can't sprout children beneath itself the way a quadtree cell does). ▸ Downward-local growth → uneven (the quadtree); upward-at-the-root growth → uniform and shallow (R-tree / B-tree).

## 5 · Encoded keys ⭐

> ⭐ **This is the approach you'll actually reach for in a Swiggy dispatch design.** Skips the custom tree entirely: **turn each (lat, long) into a single sortable key / cell ID that an ordinary B-tree already handles.** ▸ **The win: reuses infrastructure every database already ships** — no spatial extension, no special index/query/tooling.

### 5.1 Geohash — the base idea

▸ **CRUX** — geohash turns space-filling-curve math into a few lines of code. A **space-filling curve** is a single line threaded through 2-D space so that **points close on the map stay close along the line.**

▸ **Construction (hold this in your head):** divide the world into a **grid of 32 cells**, label each with one character → that's your **first character.** Take that cell, divide into **32 smaller cells** → **second character.** Recurse as deep as you want. Each extra character **zooms in.**

- ▸ **Precision ladder:** a **5-char** geohash ≈ **5 km** across; **9 chars** ≈ **5 m**.
    
- ⭐ **Under the string, it's just bits.** Each char = 1 of 32 = exactly **5 bits**. So `dr5ru` = **25 bits** grouped as 5 letters for humans. Read the same 25 bits straight through as a **number** and you get an integer with the same bits in the same order. ▸ **That's why Redis stores it as a 52-bit integer and Postgres stores it as text — both indexes behave identically.**
    
- ⭐ **The payoff — shared prefix ≈ nearby.** Two points share a prefix **only if they fell into the same cell at every level that prefix covers**; each extra shared char shrinks the shared cell (5 shared chars → same 5 km box; 6 → ~1 km). ▸ **That predictable locality is what lets a proximity query run on any sorted index:** `WHERE geohash LIKE 'dr5ru%'` is a **plain B-tree prefix scan** (`01d`) — same scan you'd run on any text column.
    
- ⭐ **Redis is this wearing different syntax** (`02a`): `GEOADD` interleaves lat/long into a **52-bit geohash integer**, stored as the **score in a sorted set**; a nearby query is a **`ZRANGEBYSCORE`** over those scores — the prefix-scan idea in sorted-set clothing.
    

#### 5.1a ⭐ The boundary problem + the 3×3 ring trick — the one real gotcha

> ⭐ **This is the gotcha that recurs across _every_ encoded-key system (geohash, S2, H3).** Worth knowing cold; it's cheap and it's the detail that shows you've actually used one.

- ⚠ **Problem — cell boundaries.** Two points a **metre apart** can land in **completely different cells** with **completely different prefixes** if they straddle a boundary. A rider on the edge of cell `dr5ru` has their closest driver 10 m away in neighbour `dr5rg` — a naive `LIKE 'dr5ru%'` scan **misses them entirely.**
- ⭐ **Fix — the 3×3 trick.** Compute the query point's cell, then **walk to the 8 neighbouring cells**, and **query all 9 as a unit.** Now you can't miss anyone within one cell's distance, no matter how close to a boundary.
- ▸ **Then post-filter** the 9-cell result by **exact distance** to drop the corners that are technically in-window but actually too far. ⭐ **Almost every encoded-key index in production does some version of this query-the-ring-and-post-filter dance** — it's the concrete face of §2's "candidate set, then finish exactly."

### 5.2 S2 — the spherical fix

▸ ⚠ **Geohash works beautifully for one city, but hurts across the globe** — it treats lat/long as a **flat rectangle** and the earth isn't flat. A degree of longitude ≈ 111 km at the equator, shrinking toward **zero at the poles**, so one geohash cell is a **fat square near the equator, a thin sliver up north.**

- ⭐ **Google's S2 = the spherical cousin.** Wraps the globe in a **cube**, projects onto the 6 faces → cells of **roughly equal area anywhere on earth**, each a **64-bit hierarchical ID** you can **truncate to get a parent cell.**
- ▸ Because it understands the sphere, it handles cases a flat grid mangles — e.g. a polygon crossing the **antimeridian** (the 180° line where coords wrap +180 → −180).
- ▸ **Ships underneath MongoDB's `2dsphere` index.** _(Cube-projection detail = name-drop; the takeaway is "S2 = global / equal-area / spherical.")_

### 5.3 H3 — hexagons, and the one real difference

▸ **Uber open-sourced H3** and runs it for **dispatch and surge pricing.** Twist: **hexagons instead of squares.**

- ⭐ **Why hexagons:** a square cell has **two kinds of neighbour** — 4 across edges (near), 4 across corners (farther). That lopsidedness makes _"everything within N rings of me"_ queries and heat maps messy. A **hexagon has 6 neighbours, all roughly equidistant** → much cleaner for the **ring-based analytics** Uber runs constantly. Like the others, each cell is a **64-bit hierarchical ID.**
- ⚠ **The difference that matters — H3 is NOT a space-filling curve.** Geohash and S2 lay cells **along a curve**, so a **numeric range scan** sweeps up a neighbourhood in one shot. ⚠ **H3 doesn't → close IDs aren't reliably close on the map.** Instead it gives you **cheap grid math to compute the ring of cells around any cell directly**, and you **look those exact IDs up.**

#### 5.3a The H3 dispatch pattern — the shape you want for moving points

- Snap each driver to an H3 cell (say **200 m**). Rider opens the app → take **their cell + the surrounding ring** (grab another ring out for a wider net).
- Query `WHERE h3_cell IN (list of cell IDs)` — a **plain lookup on an ordinary integer index** — then **post-filter by exact distance.**
- ⭐ **All cheap writes + cheap candidate generation, no spatial extension anywhere** — exactly the shape you want when **location updates arrive constantly.** ▸ This is the concrete Swiggy/Uber dispatch loop.

## 6 · Which should you use? — the decision to run in a room ⭐

> ⭐ **This is §3's axis, now with the tradeoffs attached.** This is what you actually say out loud.

||**Custom spatial tree**|**Encoded cells**|
|---|---|---|
|**Use when**|data is **geometric** — polygons, roads, zones; containment / intersection|data is **mostly moving points** — drivers, users, devices|
|**You get**|DB **understands shapes**; R-tree handles points, lines, polygons alike|**cheap writes + fast candidate generation**|
|⚠ **You pay**|a **spatial extension** + **pricier writes** (rebalancing rectangles is real work; **BKD is write-once** → neither loves churn)|**points only**; cell boundaries mean you **always query a ring** of neighbours, never one exact cell|
|**Default pick**|**PostGIS** (R-tree-style GiST, `02g`)|**geohash / S2 / H3** on an ordinary index|
|**Write cost**|⚠ heavier|⭐ a moving driver = **one integer update** → scales to **millions of writes/sec**|

- ⭐ **The Swiggy-shaped conclusion:** a moving driver is a **single integer update**, which is why encoded cells scale to millions of writes/sec — **so for live dispatch you'll almost always reach for encoded cells, not a tree.** Trees earn their keep only when the question is genuinely about **shapes** (does this delivery _zone_ contain this address).
- ⭐⭐ **The line that closes any proximity answer:** ⭐ **whichever you land on, the index only ever hands you candidates.** It narrows _"check every row"_ down to a small patch of the map, and **you still finish with exact distance / geometry math on that handful.** Once that clicks, **the specific library barely matters.**
- ⭐ **The three-word summary:** **Spatial tree for shapes. Encoded cells for moving points. And always, always post-filter.**

---

> ⭐ **The one thing to actually say (the whole file in four sentences):** _"Basic B-tree can't do proximity — a 1-D sort can't preserve 2-D closeness. So I'd reach for a spatial index. On Postgres, if the data is geometric (zones, roads, containment) I'd use PostGIS/GiST. If it's just moving points and I want cheap writes, I'd either encode to a geohash on a plain B-tree column, or use PostGIS points with `ST_DWithin` — and always post-filter by exact distance."_
